# Cryptographically Signed Ledgers (Blockchain Technology) to Restore Scientific Integrity

During my doctoral training, I documented irregularities in the data and methodology of a high-profile paper published by a senior author at my graduate institution. The irregularities included image duplication in a key figure, inconsistencies between the published data and the first author's own thesis, and physiological measurements whose kinetics were incompatible with the reported experimental parameters. I compiled this evidence and reported it within the University through the channels that ostensibly exist for this purpose.

The University administration and its nominally independent Ombuds office advised me to drop the matter in order to protect my path to graduation. Coincidentally public commentary appeared on PubPeer, where independent commenters raised concerns overlapping with and extending beyond those I had raised privately. Approximately 39 comments accumulated as of the time of this writing, identifying image duplication, inconsistencies with the first author's doctoral thesis, and multiple replication failures documented in subsequent literature. The senior author engaged some comments, but did not retract the paper or release the underlying raw data. Eventually the journal issued an erratum, but no retraction followed. By the time independent verification was sought, the underlying physical samples had been destroyed or could not be located, leaving only the processed data available to the public.

Every layer of the audit machinery that exists today (laboratory internal review, departmental oversight, university administration, institutional ombuds, journal editorial process, and post-publication commentary) was engaged in this case, and yet none produced a retraction, an investigation, or a data release. This is the predictable output of a system where the reporter bears all the cost, the institution bears all the reputational risk of acknowledgment, and no structural mechanism exists to force verification when the original physical evidence has been destroyed. The rational response for any junior researcher (as verified by the ombuds office and senior members at elite universities) in this position is silence, because the personal cost of pursuing a concern through these channels exceeds the career benefit under every plausible outcome.

The case described above illustrates the systemic bottleneck at the individual level, but the same dynamic operates across the entire publicly funded research system. The existing system of publicly funded science permits established researchers to extract career capital (publications, citations, grants, tenure) from every downstream party (reviewers, replicators, funders, and the public whose taxes financed the work). New scientists entering the system absorb the costs of navigating a literature polluted with unaudited false positives, and well-meaning incumbents are penalized for the additional time and transparency that carefulness requires. The system's ownership defaults, audit gaps, and incentive structures concentrate value at the top of the existing hierarchy, while distributing the consequences of unreliable findings across the entire scientific labor force and the public that funds it.

Yes, scientific ethical breaches are a tale as old as time; however, technological advances have amplified the problem, where even the classical p-hacking framework described by Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) and Gelman and Loken (2014), who characterized a garden of forking paths with perhaps five to twenty forks is outmatched. Modern high-throughput stacks have thousands to millions of forks per experiment, and the cost of running one more screen has collapsed roughly 100-fold in a decade (a pooled CRISPR screen that cost $50,000 in 2014 runs for a few thousand dollars today). Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg correction address the within-run multiplicity; they do not address between-run search. A lab can run twelve genome-wide screens, publish the one that yields the cleanest hit list, and report a fully corrected p-value table that is technically accurate and yet substantively meaningless. AI-assisted analysis adds a second opaque search layer where every architecture, hyperparameter, and feature-engineering choice tried during model development is a hypothesis tested against the data, and almost none are reported. The search depth that would let a reader discount the finding is structurally invisible, because no existing reporting requirement forces disclosure of the number of runs attempted, the analytic pipelines tried and discarded, or the selection criteria that determined which run became the published record.

The practices that produce unaudited false positives range across a spectrum from innocuous to deliberate. At the innocuous end, a researcher runs a screen, obtains a noisy result, re-runs it with slightly different normalization or threshold parameters, repeats this process several times until a clean hit list emerges, and publishes only the final clean run. Each decision in this sequence can feel reasonable to the person making it; the cumulative effect is publication of a finding whose true reliability is unknowable to any reader.

At the middle of the spectrum sit practices such as selective stopping, in which data collection continues until a significance threshold is crossed and then halts; analytic flexibility, in which a researcher chooses among multiple defensible pipelines after seeing the data and reports only the pipeline that produced the desired result; and HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), in which a post-hoc hypothesis is presented as though it were predicted in advance (Kerr, 1998). Finally (and hopefully more rare) at the deliberate end sit fabrication, image or measurement manipulation, selective cohort exclusion, and suppression of contradicting data.

A graduate student or postdoc who challenges a senior investigator's analytic choices risks their training position, letters of recommendation, funding, and access to the publication record they need to compete for faculty jobs. The person best positioned to observe problematic practices is the person with the most to lose by reporting them, and institutional mechanisms consistently prioritize institutional reputation over individual complainants. 

This is systematic dispossession of the public. Federally funded research data is, by any plain reading, public property. The taxpayer funded the work and the data should be the publically available deliverable. The current system permits the principal investigator to treat that data as private property, deciding unilaterally what is released, when, in what format, and to whom. Universities and PIs should be stewards of public funds, not owners of the data those funds produce. The legal and contractual default at most U.S. research universities, under which lab data and notebooks belong to the institution with the PI holding de facto control, is itself a structural transfer of ownership from the public to the institution. When the institution owns the data, the institution decides whether to release it, whether to investigate irregularities in it, and whether to preserve it when questions arise.

The NIH 2023 Data Management and Sharing Policy nominally requires that all NIH-funded research produce a data-sharing plan and deposit data in a repository. However, enforcement is nearly nonexistent. "Data available upon reasonable request" remains the de facto standard in published papers, and empirical audits of that phrase show that requests are ignored more often than honored (Gabelica et al., 2022: 6.7% compliance with stated data-sharing commitments in a sample of 3,556 papers). The ownership default is the legal scaffolding that makes every downstream failure of audit possible. It is the mechanism by which public investment is converted into private career capital without public verification.

Current solutions only partially solve this problem. For example, preregistration only targets hypotheses, not data. A screen with 20,000 simultaneous tests cannot be meaningfully preregistered as 20,000 separate hypotheses, and the field has accepted "we ran a screen" as a sufficiently registered procedure. The forks are in the analytic pipeline run after the data lands, not in the hypothesis.

The proposed intervention is a single procurement rule, applied as a condition of federal research funding, with downstream consequences that address every layer of the problem described above.

The rule being:

No federal research funds may be used to purchase, lease, or operate scientific instruments, computational pipelines, or data-acquisition systems unless those systems produce signed, tamper-evident, append-only logs of every run, conforming to a published federal data-logging standard. Manual logging is permitted as an exception when no compliant instrument exists, provided the manual entries are signed by the operator and deposited into a public append-only ledger at the time of recording. 

Every run would be logged at the instrument with a cryptographically signed record that establishes existence, time, and content at the moment of generation, but the raw data payload would be encrypted with keys held jointly by the funding agency and the institution during a defined embargo period. During the embargo, the cryptographic signature already establishes that the run occurred and what it contained at the bit level, so any later release of the data can be audited against the original signed record for tampering or selective omission. This embargo-then-release structure is analogous to how federal grant records are handled under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). During the embargo, the signed record remains publicly verifiable, and a funding-agency request analogous to a FOIA disclosure can compel access to the raw payload for documented public-interest reasons (research misconduct allegations, congressional inquiry, criminal investigation), subject to the same statutory exemptions that apply to grant-record requests (Exemption 4 for confidential commercial information, Exemption 5 for deliberative-process material, Exemption 6 for personnel privacy). This preserves the legitimate concern that researchers should not be scooped on unpublished work in real time, while preventing the indefinite institutional retention that currently converts public data into a private asset.

Federal research funders (NIH, NSF, DOE Office of Science, USDA) represent a procurement bloc large enough that instrument vendors will not maintain non-compliant product lines for a smaller market. Three downstream consequences follow from this single rule. Universities lose the de facto ownership claim over data automatically, because the existence and content of every run is established at the moment of generation by a signed record that neither the PI nor the institution can alter after the fact, and the raw payload becomes public when the embargo expires regardless of whether the PI publishes. The embargo protects researchers from being scooped on unpublished work during the publication window, while the automatic expiration ensures that the data reaches public access on a defined schedule that no institutional decision can override. This mechanism repositions universities and researchers as stewards of public funds during the embargo period rather than as indefinite gatekeepers, and it bypasses the separate data-sharing legislation pathway that has failed to produce compliance for decades (Gabelica et al., 2022).

Once a public, signed, tamper-evident equipment log exists for every federally funded experiment, journals face strong incentive to require the log as supplementary information because reviewers will demand access to it as a condition of adequate peer review. The sequencing mirrors the ClinicalTrials.gov precedent (De Angelis et al., 2004), in which the registry existed first and journals subsequently mandated registration as a condition of publication, producing compliance through market expectation rather than through direct regulatory authority over publishers.

Independent audit then also becomes possible at scale once raw equipment logs are publicly available and cryptographically verified, because non-domain methodologists can then audit papers without permission from the journal, the PI, or the institution. Any private funder (Astera included) can fund a pool of independent methodologist-auditors who operate on the public log data. The audit function moves outside the domain's social network, eliminating the career-cost problem that currently suppresses internal policing. Importantly, even in a stripped-down form where content is not logged but instead the number of runs is logged, the system would provide a lot of information about the validity of the statistical methods for any publication.

The public that finances this research has earned the right to verify that its dollars produce reliable findings rather than search artifacts dressed as discovery. The procurement rule proposed here is the minimum return on that investment: every run is signed into the public record at runtime and tied to the grant that produced it, with the raw payload reaching full public access on a defined schedule, verifiable by anyone.
